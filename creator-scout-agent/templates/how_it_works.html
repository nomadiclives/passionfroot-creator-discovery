How this works

What the tool does, what it deliberately refuses to do, and where a human is still doing the work. Everything on this page is read from the live configuration, so it cannot drift from the model actually being applied.

In one line

You give it a campaign brief and a list of creators; it scores them against a fixed rubric, assigns each a role in the campaign, and returns a ranked shortlist. It is the evaluation half of creator sourcing, not the finding half.

The flow, and who does what

StepWho does it todayWhere it should end up
Source creators Human Agent proposes candidates, human curates
Gather metrics Human Pulled from a creator-data API
Judge the four soft dimensions Human Agent proposes with evidence, human confirms or overrides
Apply weights and gates Machine Unchanged — this stays deterministic on purpose
Assign campaign role Machine Unchanged
Approve the shortlist Human Stays human. Always.

The direction of travel is that the machine makes the first pass and the human spends their attention on the genuinely ambiguous calls — not that the machine replaces the judgement.

The scoring model

Five dimensions, each scored 1–5, combined under fixed weights. The weights are locked to the campaign spec and guarded by tests — they cannot be changed by accident, and they do not learn from feedback. They are strategy, set by a human.

Four of the five are entered by a person. That is the honest position today, and it is the single biggest cost of using this tool.

Campaign role is not the same as rank

Role answers what job does this creator do in the campaign, and comes from how ready their audience is for your product category — never from their score. A top-ranked creator can still be an awareness play.

A readiness call is evidence about one named category. It does not transfer to a brief about something else — judge the pool again instead.

Why it refuses instead of guessing

A shortlist with honest gaps is more useful than a clean one with invented numbers. Every creator evaluated stays on screen with its reason; nothing is quietly dropped, because a row that disappears looks identical to a creator nobody sourced.

StatusMeaningWhat to do
Keep Scored and shortlistedTake it to outreach
Drop Failed a gate or an exclusionRead the reason; usually final
NEEDS_REFRESH A required metric is missingSource it — never estimate it
NEEDS_REVIEW A human judgement is missing, or was made about a different category Judge it on the Judge screen
NEEDS_CALIBRATION That platform's scoring bands have not been fitted Collect real rates for it and fit bands

An empty shortlist is often the correct answer rather than a bug — most commonly when the brief names a category the pool was never judged against.

Gates run before scoring, and override it

A creator can score well and still be dropped. Engagement below the platform floor, a competitor exclusion, the wrong follower band, or no presence on a briefed platform all remove a creator regardless of how they score elsewhere.

Strengths

  • Reproducible. The same brief and pool give the same ranking every time. No gut-feel re-ordering between sessions.
  • Auditable. Every score breaks down by dimension, and every metric carries its source and the date it was sourced.
  • Corroborated. Of the 17 rows the engine and the product owner's hand-scored sheet both score, 16 reproduce exactly. Read that precisely: the sheet has 21 rows — three it had already dropped before scoring mattered, one postdates the comparison. It is agreement across the rows both parties scored, not across the whole sheet, and the engine is not a superset of the sheet's judgement. The one divergence is the sheet applying its own ladder inconsistently, pinned in a test rather than papered over.
  • Honest about gaps. The refusal states above are the point of the tool, not a limitation of it.

Limitations — read these before trusting a shortlist

  • It cannot find creators. No search, no scraping. You bring the list; the Discover screen gives you searches to run by hand.
  • It does not verify a single number. Every metric is whatever was entered. The connection to creator-data providers exists but has never made a live call.
  • It does not form judgements. Four of five dimensions are typed in by a person. The tool applies them consistently; it does not have a view.
  • Outreach angles are templates. Three pre-written lines keyed to role, with the brand name inserted. Every creator in a role gets the same line. This is a placeholder, not copy.
  • The resonance ladder saturates. {% for slug, inst in instruments.items() %}{% if inst.calibration and 'saturating' in inst.calibration %}{{ platform_labels.get(slug, slug) }}{{ ", " if not loop.last }}{% endif %}{% endfor %} use a rate ladder fed view-rate data, so most creators cluster at the top.   • Not every platform is calibrated. {% for slug, inst in instruments.items() %}{% if not inst.bands %}{{ platform_labels.get(slug, slug) }} has no fitted bands and returns NEEDS_CALIBRATION.{% endif %}{% endfor %}
  • No authentication. Anyone with the link sees the full shortlist, including commercial notes on named creators.
  • Nothing it writes survives. Uploaded lists expire after {{ retention_hours }} hours, and generated reports reset on every restart.

Where this is going

The intended design is a loop: the agent sources candidates and proposes judgements with evidence; a human reviews and corrects; those corrections are stored and fed back as worked examples so the next pass is closer; the machine applies the weights and proposes a shortlist; the human approves.

Two rules hold that loop honest. The weights never learn — strategy stays human-set, or the shortlist stops being defensible. And agreement rate is the measure: the share of proposed judgements a human accepts unchanged is how you tell the loop is working rather than merely feeling like it is.

The rubric — how a creator earns each score

The weights above say how much each dimension counts. This says what actually earns the points. Every sub-score on a shortlist is meant to be read off these ladders from evidence rather than from impression, which is what makes a ranking arguable with rather than just asserted.

A dash means no bands have been fitted for that instrument, so Dimension 3 returns NEEDS_CALIBRATION for those creators rather than inventing a boundary to score them against.

Two of these three ladders do not yet work, and it is worth knowing which. The spec writes Dimension 3 for an engagement rate — likes and comments over reach. This pool carries a view rate, views over followers, which runs an order of magnitude higher.

YouTube was refitted for that. Its bands above (35/20/10/5) look nothing like the spec's engagement ladder (4/2/1.5) because they were derived from real view-rate data, and they discriminate: the eight YouTube creators here spread across 2, 3, 4 and 5 out of 5.

TikTok and Instagram were not. Their 8/5/3 is essentially the spec's engagement ladder applied to a view rate, so every creator clears the top band — all eleven score 5/5 and the dimension does no ranking work at all on those platforms. The fix is the one already proven on YouTube, but it needs per-platform follower counts this data does not carry, so the ladder stays as the product owner set it and the gap is published rather than papered over.
